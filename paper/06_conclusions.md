# 6. What we can and cannot conclude

The clearest result is a shift toward hotter summer maxima in Spain, France
and Germany. Between 1961-1990 and 1996-2025, mean TXx anomalies rise by 2.10
°C, 2.44 °C and 1.85 °C, respectively. Sweden's estimated rise is smaller,
0.63 °C, and its period-difference interval includes zero, although its
full-record mean trend is positive with a HAC interval excluding zero. A
comparison of two periods and a trend over the full record answer different
questions and need not have the same precision.

Heat observations cross fixed historical thresholds more often in all four
countries, whichever of P75, P90, P95 or P99 defines the extreme. A shift of
the early distribution by the observed mean change reproduces much of that
increase. The upper percentiles also rise, but their extra movement relative
to the mean is imprecise. The quantile regressions similarly do not establish
a faster P95 trend than the median. The evidence therefore supports a
substantial shift, while leaving moderate changes in the upper tail
unresolved. France and Germany have wider pooled distributions, so the results
do not justify a blanket claim that shape is unchanged.

For drought, mean September SPEI-6 falls in Spain, France and Germany,
indicating a drier April-September climatic water balance. Sweden shows no
clear mean drying. Spain's increase in dry-threshold crossings persists across
all four cut-offs. The conclusion is less uniform elsewhere: France has more
crossings at P25, P10 and P5, but no increase at P1. We find no clear extra
deterioration of the P5 relative to the mean. With only 30 observations per
comparison period, this is weak evidence about the most severe droughts.

Our method combines distribution comparisons, fixed-threshold counts, a shift
benchmark, OLS trends and quantile regression. The results are descriptive
changes and associations with time. A time coefficient absorbs many processes;
it cannot isolate greenhouse gas forcing, land-use change or a particular
economic mechanism. Causal attribution would require a credible
counterfactual, such as climate-model ensembles with and without human
forcing, together with evidence on model performance and observational
uncertainty. The broader attribution evidence assessed by Seneviratne et al.
(2021) does not turn our regressions into an attribution design.

Three limits are central. National averages smooth local extremes, and these
indices do not count heatwave duration or observed crop and income losses. The
most extreme percentiles rest on very few observations, while our bootstrap
does not preserve dependence across years. Finally, the early baseline
overlaps the documented pre-1978 ERA5-Land concern; the post-1978 mean-trend
check cannot validate all early-period tail results. The practical implication
is still clear: a change in the mean can substantially raise exposure to
historically unusual conditions. The data support that conclusion more firmly
than a claim that the extreme tails have systematically become heavier.
